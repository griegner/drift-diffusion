\section{Validation by Simulation}\label{sec:simulations}
\subsection{Simulation Settings}\label{sec:simulation-settings}
The finite-sample performance of the pseudo-MLE $\hat{\bm\beta}^*$ \eqref{eq:beta_}, equivalently $\hat{\bm\theta}^*$ without covariates, and the covariance estimators $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$--$\widehat{V}_\text{SH}$ \eqref{eq:V_ar}--\eqref{eq:V_sh} was assessed via Monte Carlo simulations across three settings. In each setting, $b =$ 900 independent replications of a sequence of choice and reaction times $\bm{Y} = (Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$ of length $n =$ 1000 were simulated under the true density ($f_0$; \ref{eq:f_dep}) and fit under the working density ($f^*$; \ref{eq:f_ind}). Settings vary along two dimensions: specification ($f_0 = f^*$ or $f_0 \neq f^*$) and parameter structure (fixed or varying across trials), which together determine whether the information matrix equality $\mathcal{H}_{\bm\beta} = \mathscr{I}_{\bm\beta}$ holds and which covariance estimators are consistent (Section~\ref{sec:covariance-estimation}). The first two settings are correctly specified; the second additionally demonstrates modeling of nonstationarity through covariates. The third tests robustness to unmodeled parameter variability and temporal dependence.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \emph{Correct specification, constant parameters} ($f_0 = f^*$): a baseline correctly specified setting with parameters $a,t_0,|v|,z$ fixed across trials. Decision outcomes are $iid$, the information matrix equality holds, and all four covariance estimators are expected to be consistent (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_constant}).

    \item \emph{Correct specification, trending decision boundary} ($f_0 = f^*$): a correctly specified setting where the decision boundary increases linearly from $0.8\beta_a$ to $1.2\beta_a$ over the session, with midpoint value $\beta_a = 0.625$, while $t_0,|v|,z$ are held constant. The working model uses $a_i=\beta_a x_i$. The information matrix equality holds, and all four covariance estimators are expected to be consistent. This setting demonstrates two capabilities of the pseudo-MLE in modeling DDM parameters as functions of covariates (Section~\ref{sec:covariates}), and explicitly accounting for nonstationary parameter trends with the trial number covariate (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_linear}).

    \item \emph{Misspecification and temporal dependence} ($f_0 \neq f^*$): a misspecified setting where $a$ is fixed and $t_0,|v|,z$ switch jointly under one stationary two-state Markov chain. The working model includes stimulus direction but omits the state, estimating four constant parameters $a,t_0,|v|,z$. This constitutes misspecification of types (i) and (iii) (Section~\ref{sec:misspecification}). Only $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$ accounts for both marginal misspecification and serial dependence, and $\widehat{V}_\text{MR}$ addresses only the former (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_realistic}).

\end{itemize}

Parameter centers were taken from \citet{matzke_psychological_2009} (Table~3, means), rescaled to the present implementation: $\bar{a} = 0.625$, $\bar{t}_0 = 0.435$, $\overline{|v|} = 2.23$, $\bar{z} = 0.008$. These are the true parameters in the constant setting, with $\bar{a}$ serving as the midpoint $\beta_a$ in the trending boundary setting.

In the third setting, $a=0.625$, while $(t_0,|v|,z)$ switches between engaged $(0.395,3.56,0.008)$ and disengaged $(0.475,0.90,0.350)$ states. The engaged state combines a shorter non-decision time, stronger evidence accumulation, and a nearly neutral starting point. The disengaged state has a longer non-decision time, weaker accumulation, and rightward bias. Measured indicators of engagement, such as features extracted from behavioral videos, could help model these persistent unmodeled decision states. However, such indicators are often unavailable or omitted from the fitted model as this example demonstrates.

State differences were based on the reported across-trial variability in drift rate, non-decision time, and starting point after rescaling to our parameterization \citep{matzke_psychological_2009}. The chain starts with equal probabilities for the two states and remains in its current state with probability $0.95$, giving a mean state duration of 20 trials \citep{ashwood_mice_2022}. In all three settings, stimulus directions are independently randomized with equal probabilities and determine the drift sign. 

Pseudo-true parameters (Section~\ref{sec:misspecification}), which need not equal generating means, were approximated by fitting the working model to a 50,000-trial sequence from the third setting. Fits in this setting used the model's default initialization, with all four parameters freely estimated. We compared mean analytic standard errors and correlations with their empirical counterparts across replications.

\subsection{Simulation Results}

\subsubsection{Correct specification, constant parameters}
Under correct specification and independent trials (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_constant}), point estimates are approximately unbiased, with means within $0.09$ empirical standard deviations of the true parameters. All four covariance estimators perform comparably, giving standard errors and pairwise correlations generally close to their empirical counterparts. Mean standard errors are within $7\%$ of the empirical values across all parameters and estimators. This baseline shows that the method recovers both the decision-making parameters and their estimation uncertainty when the working model is correctly specified.

\subsubsection{Correct specification, trending decision boundary}
Including the trial number covariate accounts for the increasing boundary $a_i$ over the session (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_linear}), with results comparable to the constant-parameter setting. Estimates of the coefficient $\beta_a$ and the parameters $t_0,|v|,z$ remain approximately unbiased, with means within $0.10$ empirical standard deviations of their true values. All four covariance estimators give similar uncertainty estimates, with mean standard errors within $5.2\%$ of the empirical values. Thus, a systematic change in decision state need not compromise point or uncertainty estimation when that change is captured by the covariate model.

\subsubsection{Misspecification and temporal dependence}
Switching between persistent engaged and disengaged states (Figure~\ref{fig:sim_realistic}) introduces both marginal misspecification and temporal dependence. The constant-parameter working model omits differences between the states, while their persistence makes neighboring trials dependent. Point estimates concentrate near the pseudo-true parameters, describing the best working-model approximation to this mixture of decision processes rather than either state individually. MR accounts for marginal misspecification but still underestimates the standard error of $|v|$ by $53\%$, compared with $10\%$ for AR. AR captures much of the additional sampling variability caused by persistent state switching, although its remaining $2.4$--$9.5\%$ underestimation across parameters may partly reflect finite-sample bias. Temporal dependence also affects correlations between parameter estimates. The empirical correlation between estimates of $a$ and $|v|$ is $-0.52$, whereas SH, OP, and MR give positive mean correlations. AR recovers the negative relationship.

Ignoring this unmodeled variability and dependence can therefore give misleading confidence in the estimated decision-making parameters. Underestimated standard errors produce overly narrow confidence intervals, which can lead to undercoverage and inflated false-positive rates in hypothesis tests. By accounting for temporal dependence, AR covariances mitigate these sources of overconfidence without explicitly modeling the latent states. This improves inference about the working model's pseudo-true parameters, but does not recover the separate engaged and disengaged states since no covariate models them. These results support using AR covariance estimates for more reliable inference in realistic behavioral data.
