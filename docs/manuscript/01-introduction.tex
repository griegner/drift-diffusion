\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Perceptual Decision Making in Naturalistic Settings}

Perceptual decision making is the fundamental process of making choices based on sensory information. While often studied in static contexts, in naturalistic settings this behavior is rarely stable; rather, choice behavior relies on an interplay between multiple interleaved strategies \citep{ashwood_mice_2022} and is richly structured in time \citep{urai_structure_2025, danskin_exponential_2023}. The dominant theoretical framework for understanding these two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) decisions is the sequential sampling model, which postulates that decisions are made upon the sequential analysis of evidence \citep{shadlen_speed_2006, asadpour_rodents_2024}. This framework is supported by substantial neural evidence \citep{shadlen_speed_2006, steinemann_direct_2024} and underlying computational principles \citep{bogacz_physics_2006} that describe the noisy temporal accumulation of evidence leading to a commitment to a choice \citep{asadpour_rodents_2024, deakin_support_2024}.

\subsection{The Drift Diffusion Model (DDM)}

The Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) parameterizes this unobserved, latent decision-making process. It posits that noisy sensory evidence is integrated over time until it reaches a decision threshold \citep{ratcliff_theory_1978, shevinsky_interaction_2019}, providing a single mechanism that simultaneously explains which choice is made and how long it takes to make it. The model translates latent cognitive variables into observed behavior via four main parameters: the rate of evidence accumulation (drift rate $v$), the speed-accuracy tradeoff determined by decision thresholds (decision bound $a$), the initial bias (starting point $z$), and the non-decision time ($t_0$) required for sensory and motor processing \citep{ratcliff_individual_2015, myers_practical_2022, deakin_support_2024}.

\subsection{Decision Making Over Time}
Experimental data typically consists of a sequence of choice and reaction time trials collected over time. While standard analyses often treat these as independent, decisions are not isolated events, but are embedded in a sequence of choices \citep{urai_structure_2025}.

First, the decision state/strategy is not constant; it changes over time with changing internal and external states across multiple timescales. Standard measures of decision making mask the presence of dramatic changes in decision strategies across trials \citep{ashwood_mice_2022}. For example, subjects may alternate between ``engaged'' states and ``biased or weakly stimulus-dependent strategies'' \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, mohammadi_identifying_2025}. As noted by \citet{urai_structure_2025}, ``there is probably no such thing as stable behavior.'' Furthermore, distinct forms of behavioral variability may distinguish decision-making processes between species, such as humans and rats \citep{nguyen_different_2022, shevinsky_interaction_2019}.

Second, decisions are influenced by the outcomes of previous choices (sequential effects). Animals use past experience to guide future choices, often integrating history information across multiple timescales \citep{danskin_exponential_2023, nguyen_optimizing_2019, busse_detection_2011}. These history biases are prevalent in human, monkey, and rodent perceptual decision-making \citep{urai_choice_2019, busse_detection_2011, trevino_response_2022}. Taken together, decision making adapts over time, using the past to guide the present, resulting in trial-to-trial variability that is richly structured in time \citep{urai_structure_2025}.

Much of this non-stationarity is structured rather than random. Parameters may trend within a session as fatigue accumulates, or shift between sessions as animals learn. When such trends can be linked to observable quantities (trial number, time of day, stimulus or reward history), they can be explicitly modeled as functions of covariates. By absorbing structured non-stationarity into the covariate model, the residual variability is plausibly stable over time, enabling principled inference even in naturalistic experiments.

\subsection{Current Estimation Methods and Their Limitations}

For DDM fitting, there is usually the implicit assumption that, in the absence of experimental manipulations, the decision making process remains constant over time with fixed parameters $a, z, t_0, v$. Trial order is ignored, treating decision outcomes as independent and identically distributed ($iid$), a condition often violated in real data \citep{vincent_recurrent_2017, urai_structure_2025}. If parameters are allowed to vary (e.g., the 7-parameter DDM), they do so as unstructured random variability, ignoring trial order and the possibility that systematic trends, such as learning curves or circadian rhythms, may be linked to observable covariates \citep{ratcliff_parameter_2013}. This discounts the rich temporal structure from which decision-making occurs and ignores the possibility that an animal makes abrupt changes in decision-making strategy \citep{urai_structure_2025, ashwood_mice_2022}. Furthermore, these across-trial variability parameters are inherently hard to estimate and often show poor recovery because of this unidentifiability \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, lerche_model_2016, nguyen_different_2022}.

Current inference methods are limited in the following ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Point estimates of DDM parameters rarely include their corresponding uncertainties (standard errors) \citep{wiecki_hddm_2013, boehm_estimating_2018}.
    \item If uncertainties are estimated, they depend on asymptotic approximations assuming ideal conditions ($iid$) that are rarely met in practice \citep{vincent_recurrent_2017}.
    \item Robust estimation often relies on computationally expensive methods like Bayesian MCMC or resampling \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, vincent_recurrent_2017}.
    \item The robustness of inference to simplifying assumptions (misspecifications such as unmodeled parameter variability and temporal correlations) is rarely addressed. Under model misspecification, standard errors underestimate true parameter uncertainty, leading to overconfident and invalid inferences \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, urai_structure_2025, hato_levy_2025}.
\end{itemize}

These limitations constrain hypothesis testing to compare parameters across conditions or detect meaningful temporal changes in decision states, particularly limiting studies of dynamic decision processes in naturalistic settings where brain and behavior are in constant flux \citep{shevinsky_interaction_2019}.

\subsection{Proposed Solutions}

To address these issues, we consider how to explicitly allow for model misspecification and time dependence in order to conduct valid inference. We propose modeling DDM parameters as functions of observed covariates (such as stimulus strength, trial number, or time of day) via a link function. This serves two purposes: it captures how external factors influence each decision, and it provides a principled mechanism for non-stationary behavior. Systematic parameter trends that would otherwise violate stationarity are absorbed into the covariate model, leaving residual variability that is plausibly stable over time. Stationarity is therefore a requirement on the joint process of covariates and outcomes, not on the raw decision outcomes alone. This distinction substantially broadens the scope of experiments to which the framework applies.

We introduce a computationally efficient pseudo-Maximum Likelihood Estimation (pseudo-MLE) approach to estimate DDM parameters and covariate effects, and quantify their uncertainties without requiring correct specification or trial independence. We show that the proposed method is consistent and asymptotically normal, and provide parameter recovery simulations for both point estimates and their uncertainties. In applications to rat decision making, we demonstrate that, even under constant experimental conditions, decision states change over time, DDM parameters change over many timescales in ways that are larger than their uncertainty.
