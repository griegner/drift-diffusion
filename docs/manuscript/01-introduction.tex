\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Perceptual Decision Making in Naturalistic Settings}
Perceptual decision making is the process of making decisions based on sensory information. While often studied in highly controlled experiments, in naturalistic settings these decisions are rarely stable; rather, they emerge from the interplay between multiple interleaved decision states that are richly structured in time \citep{roy_efficient_2018, roy_extracting_2021, ashwood_mice_2022, duffy_disentangling_2025, urai_structure_2025, danskin_exponential_2023, perquin_temporal_2023, cochrane_multiple_2023}. The dominant theoretical models of these two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) decisions are sequential sampling models, which postulate that decisions are made upon the sequential analysis of evidence \citep{shadlen_speed_2006, asadpour_rodents_2024}. These models are supported by substantial neural evidence and underlying computational principles that describe the noisy accumulation of evidence leading to a choice \citep{shadlen_speed_2006, steinemann_direct_2024, bogacz_physics_2006, asadpour_rodents_2024, deakin_support_2024}.

\subsection{The Drift Diffusion Model (DDM)}
We consider the DDM, a sequential sampling model with a long history of describing decision making. Its latent diffusion process accumulates noisy sensory evidence until it reaches a decision bound, explaining both the choice and the time taken to make it \citep{stone_models_1960, ratcliff_theory_1978, shadlen_neural_2001}. The model translates the latent diffusion process into observed behavior via parameters that together define the \emph{decision-making state}; elsewhere in the literature this is referred to as the decision-making strategy \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, roy_efficient_2018, roy_extracting_2021}. The DDM and its parameters are defined in Section~\ref{sec:ddm}.

\subsection{Decision Making Over Time}
The decision state is not constant; it changes over time with internal and external environments across timescales ranging from milliseconds to hours to days and beyond \citep{duffy_disentangling_2025, urai_choice_2019, cochrane_multiple_2023, shevinsky_interaction_2019, schumacher_neural_2023, schumacher_validation_2025}. Subjects adapt to these changing environments by adjusting their internal decision states, and these latent states in turn shape observed decision outcomes. Although standard measures of decision making often mask the temporal structure of these changes, recent approaches have begun to recover these time-varying dynamics by inferring parameter trajectories directly from behavioral data \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, gunawan_time-evolving_2022, kucharsky_hidden_2021, maggi_tracking_2024, alister_diffusion_2026}. For example, subjects may alternate between discrete engaged stimulus-dependent states and disengaged stimulus-independent states \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, mohammadi_identifying_2025, duffy_disentangling_2025, senne_state-dependent_2025} or drift slowly between continuous states \citep{vloeberghs_bayesian_2025, roy_extracting_2021, roy_efficient_2018}, or a combination of both \citep{schumacher_neural_2023, schumacher_validation_2025}. As noted by \citet{urai_structure_2025} in the context of decision making, ``there is probably no such thing as stable behavior.''

Moreover, decisions are history dependent, influenced by sequential effects including the outcomes of previous choices \citep{frund_quantifying_2014, gao_sequential_2009}. Subjects use past experience to guide future choices, often integrating history information across multiple timescales \citep{danskin_exponential_2023, nguyen_optimizing_2019, busse_detection_2011, miletic_explaining_2025}. This kind of history dependence can include effects of reinforcement learning, in which rewarded actions shape subsequent choices \citep{pedersen_drift_2017, fontanesi_reinforcement_2019, fengler_beyond_2022}, as well as reward-independent sequential biases \citep{frund_quantifying_2014, gao_sequential_2009}. These forms of temporal structure are prevalent across perceptual decision making in humans, monkeys, and rodents \citep{urai_choice_2019, busse_detection_2011, trevino_response_2022, braun_adaptive_2018, gupta_trial-history_2024}. Humans may also exhibit forms of variability similar to those observed in rodents, differing only in the relative variability of different decision-making parameters \citep{nguyen_different_2022, shevinsky_interaction_2019}. Together, these findings demonstrate that decision making across species depends on past experience and exhibits trial-to-trial variability structured in time.

\subsection{Current Estimation Methods and Their Limitations}
Common DDM specifications assume independent trials with either constant parameters or independent across-trial parameter variability, as in the conventional seven-parameter DDM \citep{ratcliff_modeling_1998, ratcliff_parameter_2013}. Although the latter accommodates parameter variability, it does not capture how this variability is structured over time. Other approaches explicitly model systematic changes through covariates \citep{cochrane_multiple_2023, alister_diffusion_2026}, latent states \citep{gunawan_time-evolving_2022, kucharsky_hidden_2021}, or history-dependent learning \citep{miletic_explaining_2025, pedersen_drift_2017, fontanesi_reinforcement_2019}. However, these models may still leave some variability or temporal dependence unexplained. Moreover, across-trial variability parameters can be difficult to estimate reliably, exhibiting poor recovery and weak test-retest reliability \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, lerche_model_2016, ratcliff_individual_2015, ratcliff_internal_2018}.

Bayesian methods quantify uncertainty through posterior distributions, but sampling these distributions can be computationally expensive for complex models or repeated fits \citep{wiecki_hddm_2013, fengler_hssm_2026}. More generally, current inference methods face four limitations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Some fitting methods provide point estimates without corresponding uncertainty estimates \citep{shinn_flexible_2020, boehm_estimating_2018}.
    \item When uncertainties are reported, their reliability depends on assumptions about the fitted model. Both asymptotic confidence intervals and Bayesian credible intervals can have incorrect coverage under model misspecification \citep{white_maximum_1982, shaby_open-faced_2014}.
    \item Fitting complex DDMs and quantifying their uncertainties can be computationally expensive, particularly for large datasets or analyses requiring many repeated fits \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, moens_recurrent_2018, wiecki_hddm_2013, fengler_hssm_2026}.
    \item Unmodeled parameter variability and temporal dependence can compromise uncertainty estimates, producing confidence intervals with incorrect coverage even when some sources of variability are explicitly modeled.
\end{itemize}

Together, these limitations constrain reliable inference about DDM parameters and the detection of meaningful temporal changes in decision-making states, particularly when some sources of variability and dependence remain unmodeled.

\subsection{Proposed Solutions}
We consider the case of decision making over time, which introduces dependence and complicates inference. For example, parameters may trend within a session as satiety or fatigue accumulates, or drift between sessions as subjects learn. To address this, we explicitly allow for time dependence and model misspecification in order to conduct valid inference. Building on existing covariate-based DDM approaches, we model DDM parameters as functions of observed covariates (such as stimulus strength or trial-in-session) via link functions. Our contribution combines covariate modeling with computationally efficient uncertainty estimates that account for misspecification and unmodeled temporal dependence under the stated assumptions. This captures how external factors influence each decision and provides a principled mechanism for nonstationary behavior. Systematic parameter trends that would otherwise violate stationarity are explained by the covariate model, leaving remaining variability that is plausibly stable over time. Stationarity is therefore a requirement on the joint process of covariates and outcomes, not on the observed decision outcomes alone. This distinction substantially broadens the scope of experiments to which the method applies.

We introduce a computationally efficient pseudo-maximum likelihood estimation (pseudo-MLE) method for estimating DDM parameters and covariate effects, together with analytical uncertainty estimates that remain valid under model misspecification and temporal dependence. This working-likelihood and sandwich-covariance framework could extend to Bayesian and other DDM inference methods, including sandwich-adjusted MCMC \citep{shaby_open-faced_2014}, with long-run score covariance accounting for dependent trials \citep{newey_simple_1987}. Our frequentist implementation provides fast inference under the stated large-sample assumptions.

Numbered equations indicate key expressions referenced throughout, while the others provide intermediate steps that can be skipped without loss of continuity. We implemented all described methods in Python except where other packages are explicitly cited.
