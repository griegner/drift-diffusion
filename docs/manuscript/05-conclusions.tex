\section{Practical Considerations}\label{sec:practical-consideration}

\subsection*{Covariate-Dependent Parameters and Across-Trial Variability}

The four-parameter DDM allows parameters to vary across trials through covariates \eqref{eq:g}. When parameters are assumed constant, the model can fail to reproduce differences in the relative speeds of correct and error responses observed in empirical human, monkey, and rat reaction time data \citep{ratcliff_diffusion_2008, ratcliff_diffusion_2016, roitman_response_2002, shevinsky_interaction_2019}. Historically, this motivated the seven-parameter DDM, which adds independent across-trial variability in drift rate, starting point, and non-decision time \citep{ratcliff_theory_1978, ratcliff_modeling_1998, ratcliff_diffusion_2008}. We compare the two approaches in reproducing slow errors (Figure~\ref{fig:pupil-drift}) and in quantifying uncertainty under realistic misspecification and temporal dependence (Figure~\ref{fig:mle-mcmc}). In both comparisons, the four-parameter model is fitted by pseudo-MLE \eqref{eq:beta_}, and the seven-parameter model by Bayesian MCMC in \emph{HSSM} v0.5.0 \citep{fengler_hssm_2026}, the successor to \emph{HDDM} \citep{wiecki_hddm_2013}, using four chains with 1000 tuning iterations and 12{,}000 retained samples each. Across-trial variability parameters describe a mixture of trial-level values, whereas covariates specify how those values change across trials.

Figure~\ref{fig:pupil-drift} illustrates this distinction using $n=1000$ simulated trials. Drift follows $v_i = c_i(\beta_{v,0} + \beta_{v,1}p_i)$, where signed coherence $c_i \in \{-1,+1\}$ determines the correct response direction and centered pupil diameter $p_i$ varies slowly across trials as a Gaussian process, serving as a proxy for arousal. The remaining parameters are fixed at the values in Section~\ref{sec:simulation-settings}. Although only drift rate depends on pupil diameter in the generating model, the covariate-dependent fit allows pupil effects on all four DDM parameters, reflecting the realistic setting in which the source of parameter variability is unknown. As pupil-linked drift magnitude decreases, responses become slower and less accurate. Consequently, longer reaction times contain a greater proportion of low-drift trials, producing a declining conditional accuracy function. The constant four-parameter model fails to reproduce this pattern, whereas both the pupil-dependent four-parameter model and the seven-parameter model approximate it, with the pupil-dependent fit closer to the true curve. However, the seven-parameter model assumes conditionally independent drifts $v_i \mid c_i \sim \mathcal{N}(c_i\mu_v,s_v^2)$, so it captures the marginal variability in drift but not its temporal structure, which the covariate-dependent model recovers together with its uncertainty.

Slow errors therefore do not by themselves rule out a conditional four-parameter DDM. When parameter variability is systematically related to measured covariates, a conditional four-parameter model can reproduce the same qualitative reaction-time pattern while quantifying how those covariates relate to the underlying decision-making state. Covariate modeling is useful when variability can be linked to measured quantities such as arousal, fatigue, learning, or experimental conditions. Here, measured pupil diameter fully explains drift variability through the link function \eqref{eq:g}. Robust covariance estimates such as $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$ \eqref{eq:V_ar} account for misspecification in the standard errors but do not remove it, so unmodeled variability, latent states, or alternative within-trial mechanisms may require models that explicitly represent these features.

\subsection*{Comparison with the Seven-Parameter DDM Fitted by MCMC}
Figure~\ref{fig:pupil-drift} shows that the seven-parameter model can reproduce slow errors, but it treats parameter variability as independent across trials. We therefore compare the uncertainty estimates of both models in a simulation that combines the sources of variability considered so far in the present paper (Figure~\ref{fig:mle-mcmc}A). The decision boundary increases linearly over the session as in Figure~\ref{fig:sim_linear}, drift magnitude varies with pupil diameter as in Figure~\ref{fig:pupil-drift}, and the starting point switches between two persistent states as in Figure~\ref{fig:sim_realistic}, with switching probability 0.05 per trial. The state is omitted from both fitted models, introducing misspecification and temporal dependence. Each of $b=500$ replications simulates one session of $n=1000$ trials. The four-parameter model includes the session-time and pupil covariates, with standard errors from $\widehat{V}_\text{SH}$ \eqref{eq:V_sh} and $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$ \eqref{eq:V_ar}. The seven-parameter model includes stimulus direction but no other covariates, so it can absorb the trend, pupil, and state effects only as independent across-trial variability in $t_0$, $v$, and $z$. We compare both models on the boundary at mid-session $\bar{a}$, non-decision time $t_0$, drift magnitude at mean pupil diameter $\bar{v}$, and starting point $z$.

Theoretically, parameters and standard errors are different when comparing MLE (Frequentist) and MCMC (Bayesian) methods, as the latter defines a prior on the parameter space. Additionally, MCMC estimates the full posterior distribution from which point estimates (marginal posterior means) and their standard errors (marginal posterior standard deviations) are calculated. Yet the influence of the prior diminishes at large samples, and asymptotically, the posterior converges to a normal distribution centered on the MLE with covariance $\mathscr{I}_{\bm\theta}^{-1}/n$, where $\mathscr{I}_{\bm\theta}$ is the per-trial Fisher information defined in Section~\ref{sec:properties} \citep[Theorem~10.1]{van_der_vaart_asymptotic_1998}; the same as the MLE under $iid$ trials and correct specification. This theorem links MLE and MCMC inference at large samples, and therefore we can compare the parameter and standard error estimates of these methods. Under misspecification, however, the posterior covariance approaches the inverse Hessian, estimated for the pseudo-MLE by $\widehat{V}_\text{SH}$ \eqref{eq:V_sh}, rather than the sandwich covariance estimated by $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$ \eqref{eq:V_ar}, and so accounts for neither misspecification nor serial dependence \citep{shaby_open-faced_2014}.

Point estimates from both models are nearly unbiased, within 0.4\% (Figure~\ref{fig:mle-mcmc}C), but their standard errors differ (Figure~\ref{fig:mle-mcmc}D,E). For the starting point $z$, which switches with the unmodeled state, $\widehat{V}_\text{SH}$ underestimates the standard error of the pseudo-MLE by 39\%, giving 77\% coverage of 95\% confidence intervals. $\widehat{V}_\text{AR}$ reduces this bias to 9\% and raises coverage to 93\%; across all four parameters, it underestimates standard errors by 3--10\%, with 91--93\% coverage. Consistent with the theory above, the seven-parameter posterior standard deviations, like $\widehat{V}_\text{SH}$, underestimate the standard error of $z$ by 37\%, giving 79\% coverage of 95\% credible intervals, and overestimate that of $t_0$ by 21\%. Thus, although the seven-parameter model accounts for across-trial variability, its uncertainty estimates remain biased when that variability persists over many trials rather than varying independently from trial to trial.

The cost of MCMC limited this comparison to $n=1000$ trials (Figure~\ref{fig:mle-mcmc}B). On an 11-core macOS machine, a seven-parameter fit took on average 9 minutes at $n=1000$ and 86 minutes at $n=10{,}000$, whereas the four-parameter pseudo-MLE took 3 and 114 seconds ($180\times$ and $45\times$ faster). The same simulation experiment at $n=10{,}000$ would require a runtime of 15 days with MCMC, compared with under two hours with pseudo-MLE. Unlike the closed-form four-parameter density \eqref{eq:f}, the seven-parameter likelihood must be integrated numerically over across-trial variability at every evaluation. This cost also limits analyses of experiments with many subjects, sessions, or trials.

That said, MCMC offers much that our method does not. Most notably, \emph{HSSM} can approximate likelihoods when no analytical form exists, differentiable or not \citep{fengler_hssm_2026, fengler_beyond_2022, fengler_likelihood_2021}. This allows for a wide class of DDMs beyond the four-parameter model, including the seven-parameter model \citep{ratcliff_modeling_1998, ratcliff_estimating_2002}, collapsing-bound model \citep{drugowitsch_cost_2012}, L\'{e}vy-flight and Cauchy-flight models \citep{voss_sequential_2019}. It can also implement hierarchical structures to pool data across subjects or sessions, improving estimation accuracy when individual data is sparse. Sandwich adjustments could extend robust uncertainty to these models \citep{shaby_open-faced_2014}. Overall, the covariate-dependent four-parameter DDM fitted by pseudo-MLE is appropriate when the research question concerns how measured covariates relate to decision-making parameters, particularly when trials are temporally dependent or many fits are required, as in simulation studies or analyses of many subjects, sessions, or time windows. Richer DDMs fitted by MCMC are preferable when across-trial variability or alternative within-trial mechanisms, such as collapsing bounds, are themselves of interest, or when sparse individual data require hierarchical pooling.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
When parameter variability and temporal dependence are unmodeled, standard errors and credible intervals from DDMs that treat trials as independent depend on unjustified assumptions and can be misleading, in particular tending to underestimate uncertainty \citep{urai_structure_2025, moens_recurrent_2018}. This paper formalizes and evaluates a pseudo-MLE method that provides analytically tractable uncertainty quantification for DDM parameters while remaining valid under temporal dependence and unmodeled parameter variability, and extending to settings where parameters are explicitly modeled as functions of covariates.

\subsubsection*{Theoretical Contributions}
We establish that the pseudo-MLE converges to its pseudo-true coefficients and is asymptotically normal under strict stationarity and strong mixing. The key insight is that stationarity applies to the joint process of covariates and outcomes, not to the decision outcomes alone. By explicitly modeling structured nonstationarity --- such as session-level trends, circadian rhythms, and learning effects --- valid point estimates and uncertainties are recovered even in nonstationary examples, substantially broadening the scope of settings to which the method applies. The resulting sandwich-form covariance estimators \citep{newey_simple_1987, hansen_probability_2022} correct for the failure of the information matrix equality under possible misspecification, providing valid standard errors and confidence intervals even when the working model is an approximation of the true data-generating process.

\subsubsection*{Simulation Results}
Monte Carlo simulations confirm that under correct specification, with either constant or covariate-driven nonstationary parameters, point estimates are approximately unbiased and covariances are accurately recovered, consistent with prior DDM parameter recovery studies \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, matzke_psychological_2009}. With persistent unmodeled decision states, correcting marginal misspecification alone is insufficient. Autocorrelation-robust covariances reduce standard-error bias for drift magnitude and recover the negative correlation between boundary and drift magnitude estimates. In a realistic setting combining a trending boundary, pupil-linked drift, and persistent unmodeled states, posterior standard deviations from a seven-parameter DDM fitted by MCMC underestimate the standard error of the state-dependent starting point by 37\%, whereas autocorrelation-robust standard errors are within 10\% at a small fraction of the computational cost. Robust covariance estimation is therefore advisable whenever misspecification cannot be ruled out, which \citet{urai_structure_2025} cautions is the norm in decision-making experiments.

More broadly, when measured covariates explain parameter variability, modeling those relationships can reveal temporal structure that is lost when variability is treated solely as independent noise \citep{duffy_disentangling_2025, urai_structure_2025, roy_efficient_2018, roy_extracting_2021}. In the seven-parameter DDM, across-trial variability is introduced in non-decision time $t_0$, drift rate $v$, and starting point $z$, while boundary height $a$ remains constant \citep{boehm_estimating_2018, lerche_model_2016, ratcliff_internal_2018}. Covariate modeling allows boundary height and starting point to vary separately with observed predictors. More generally, when structured variability can be tied to observable quantities, covariate modeling converts a source of inferential noise into interpretable signal about how decision states change over time.

\subsubsection*{Data Analysis Results}
Applied to empirical data from a single rat performing a visual motion discrimination task \citep{reinagel_speed_2013}, the method revealed systematic multi-timescale structure in DDM parameters invisible to standard analyses. At the between-day timescale, increasing decision boundaries and drift rate coefficients across the first 35 days could be a practice effect as often reported \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, mohammadi_identifying_2025, cochrane_multiple_2023, alister_diffusion_2026}. Notably, parameters continued to vary well beyond this initial period, suggesting that decision states change even in well-trained animals. Comparing Figure~\ref{fig:rat-ddm}A,C further shows that explicitly modeling within-day nonstationary trends yielded broadly similar between-day point estimates and interval widths. Standard errors were on average 1.35\% smaller when trial-in-day covariates were included. Within a day, decision boundaries and non-decision times increased overall, and drift rate coefficients declined, consistent with increasing satiety and fatigue. Markedly lower boundaries and faster responses in the first 20 trials might reflect heightened thirst at the start of the day. These findings show that even under mostly constant experimental conditions, DDM parameters varied substantially relative to their uncertainties, changes that cannot be inferred from point estimates alone and that methods ignoring trial order would attribute to noise. Short-lag autocorrelations in reaction times, boundary height, and starting point indicated remaining day-to-day dependence \citep{urai_choice_2019, busse_detection_2011, danskin_exponential_2023, frund_quantifying_2014, gao_sequential_2009}.

\subsubsection*{Limitations and Future Directions}
While misspecification-robust covariances reduce inferential errors, they cannot replace the explicit modeling of known sources of variability. For instance, history-dependent biases that drive sequential dependencies were not modeled here, as they were in previous work \citep{nguyen_optimizing_2019, urai_choice_2019, braun_adaptive_2018, gupta_trial-history_2024, miletic_explaining_2025}. Methodologically, the large-sample theory underlying our pseudo-MLE approach requires that the joint process of covariates and outcomes be strictly stationary and strongly mixing. These conditions are difficult to verify in practice, although our application to rat decision making suggests that realistic within-day nonstationarity did not substantively distort the between-day point estimates in this example (Figure~\ref{fig:rat-ddm}A,C). Explicitly modeling it yielded similar uncertainty estimates. Empirically, applying the method to a single rat under fixed stimulus conditions was meant as a demonstration, and the results presented are specific to this example and not expected to generalize across different rats or task designs. We analyzed example covariates by way of illustration of the method, but did not attempt to exhaust the many potentially explanatory covariates in this dataset. Finally, because the current method estimates fixed coefficients within each fitted window, it characterizes continuous parameter shifts but cannot capture the abrupt, discrete strategy switches that latent-state models are specifically designed to detect \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, mohammadi_identifying_2025, kucharsky_hidden_2021, senne_state-dependent_2025}.

Future work could address these limitations in three primary ways. First, history-dependent biases and species-specific traits \citep{nguyen_different_2022, shevinsky_interaction_2019, li_dynamic_2024, pagan_individual_2025} are better addressed by incorporating them as covariates into the underlying model. Extending the method to large, multi-subject datasets would then enable the comparison of changing decision dynamics across different subjects, timescales, and species \citep{asadpour_rodents_2024, danskin_exponential_2023, nguyen_different_2022}. Second, testing additional covariates derived from external behavioral metrics, such as pose tracking \citep{mathis_deeplabcut_2018} or arousal signals \citep{murphy_pupil-linked_2014}, would give richer insights into how physical context shapes evidence accumulation. Linking these physical signals directly to DDM parameters would further disentangle structured variability explained by observed covariates and unmodeled variability that requires robust inference. Finally, our pseudo-MLE approach could be combined with latent-state models, such as hidden Markov models for discrete state switches \citep{ashwood_mice_2022, mohammadi_identifying_2025, kucharsky_hidden_2021, senne_state-dependent_2025}, autoregressive processes for gradual drift \citep{urai_choice_2019, vloeberghs_bayesian_2025}, transition models that combine discrete switches with continuous drift \citep{schumacher_neural_2023, schumacher_validation_2025}, or reinforcement learning for history effects \citep{pedersen_drift_2017, fontanesi_reinforcement_2019, fengler_beyond_2022}. Together, these extensions could jointly account for structured nonstationarity, gradual parameter drift, and discrete state changes, more fully capturing the broad spectrum of temporal variability inherent in sequential decision making.

In conclusion, this work establishes a methodological foundation for studying dynamic decision making in naturalistic settings, in theory and applications. By relaxing the standard assumptions of correct specification and trial independence, and by grounding stationarity in the joint process of covariates and outcomes rather than in decision outcomes alone, the pseudo-MLE method enables principled and computationally efficient inference about sequential decision making in naturalistic experiments.
